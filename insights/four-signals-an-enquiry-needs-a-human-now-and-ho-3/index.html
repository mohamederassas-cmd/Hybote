Four signals an enquiry needs a human now, and how to hand over

A simple handover rule, built on four clear signals, helps any business that lives on enquiries bring the right person into the conversation at the right moment.

2026-11-02 · Mohamed Erassas · 4 min read

Handover problems are usually timing problems

Most businesses that live on enquiries do not lose buyers because of the tool they use. They lose them because the right person joins the conversation too early or too late. Hand over too early and your most experienced people spend their day chasing browsers. Hand over too late and a ready buyer has already spoken to whoever answered first.

The examples in this article come from real estate brokerages working on WhatsApp. The logic applies to any business where the first conversation shapes the sale: a hotel sales office, a car dealership, a school admissions team, a premium service firm.

Timing matters more than many teams assume. In an anonymous benchmark of 139 buyer enquiries sent to real estate brokerages across the GCC, 94 % of them outside office hours, 61 % of all enquiries got no reply during our test. When brokerages did reply, the median first response took 9.4 hours. WhatsApp performed better than email, with 63 % replying, yet the median first response there was still 8.7 hours. A handover rule will not fix slow first replies by itself. Once a conversation is running, though, it decides whether a warm buyer stays warm.

The four signals

One signal is enough. No scoring sheet, no debate in the team chat, no waiting for the morning meeting. If any of the following appears, a person with authority joins the conversation now.

  • **The buyer asks for a date.** "Can I see it on Saturday?" is intent, not curiosity. In other industries this is a request for a site visit, a test drive or a meeting.
  • **The buyer shares a firm budget and a clear timeline.** Someone who names a budget and a move date has done the thinking. Put a senior person in the chat while that thinking is fresh.
  • **The buyer wants to negotiate.** Discounts, payment plans, staged terms. Only someone with authority should answer. Improvised numbers cost trust, and often margin.
  • **The buyer asks whether they are talking to a person.** This is the one teams miss most often.

Why the fourth signal gets missed

The first three signals are about the deal, so sales teams notice them. The fourth is about the relationship, and it often arrives quietly, in the middle of an otherwise routine exchange.

When a buyer asks "Am I talking to a person?", they are telling you something important. They are ready to invest more in this conversation, or they are starting to feel uneasy. In both cases the right response is the same: answer honestly, then hand over at once.

A sentence such as "You are chatting with our digital assistant. I am connecting you with a senior agent now." does two things. It keeps the buyer's trust, and it shows that someone senior is available. For a premium brand, honesty here is not optional. In hospitality, guests tend to accept a short wait far more easily than the feeling that they were misled. Buyers of high value products are no different.

Make the handover clean

A handover only helps if the person receiving it does not have to start again. Whoever joins should arrive with the context already in hand: what the buyer is interested in, their preferred times, any budget or timeline they mentioned, and the conversation so far.

Example: picture a buyer who asks about a two bedroom apartment, mentions a move in three months and asks for a Saturday viewing. A clean handover gives the senior agent the unit, the timeline and the requested day in a single message. The agent's first line can confirm a time instead of asking which property the buyer meant. Nobody asks the buyer anything twice.

The rule works the same whether the first reply comes from an AI assistant or a junior colleague. The job of the first reply is to qualify. The job of your senior person is to close. When those roles are clear, the first responder does not need to guess when to step aside, and the senior team does not need to read every chat to find the ones that matter.

What to do this week

Write the four signals down in plain language and pin them where your team works, whether that is a sales group, a shared inbox or the wall behind the reception desk.

Decide who receives a handover, both during office hours and outside them. A signal that appears at 10 pm still needs a named person, or at least a clear promise of when that person will reply.

Agree on one honest sentence for the moment a buyer asks whether they are talking to a person, and make sure everyone who answers enquiries uses it.

Once a week, look at the conversations where a handover came late. For each one, ask a single question: which signal was missed? Over a few weeks, the pattern usually becomes clear, and the fix is often a small change in habit rather than a new tool.
